Introduction Background People turn to the internet when they have medical questions. That is not going to change. What changes is whether the information they find is grounded in verified sources or assembled from statistical patterns that happen to sound authoritative. Most consumer health websites fall into the second category --- written for the median reader, not the specific person asking, and updated infrequently if at all. ai-powered qa systems have existed in various forms since the 1970s. MYCIN~ shortliffe1974mycin, developed at Stanford, used hand-coded rules to recommend antibiotic treatments for blood infections. Within its narrow domain it performed well, but maintaining the rule base required continuous expert input and the system could not generalise beyond what its authors had explicitly encoded. That same limitation --- that medical knowledge is too large and changes too quickly to encode manually --- is precisely what motivates the retrieval-based approach taken in this project. Modern llm systems solve the knowledge encoding problem but introduce a different one. A model trained on web-scale text has encountered a large volume of medical content and can produce fluent, specific-sounding answers. The difficulty is that it cannot reliably distinguish between what it knows accurately and what it has reconstructed from training distributions. rag~ lewis2020rag, proposed by Lewis et al. fig:rag_pipeline illustrates the two-stage flow. figure[!ht] centering images/rag_pipeline_final.png The RAG pipeline: a retriever fetches relevant documents from the knowledge base, which are then passed as context to the language model for grounded generation. fig:rag_pipeline figure There is a practical constraint that runs through every design decision in this project. Running a 7B-parameter model on a remote server costs money and routes user queries through a third party. For medical questions, that is a genuine privacy concern. TinyLlama 1.1B~ zhang2024tinyllama is small enough to run on cpu hardware that most students and small clinics already own. This system uses TinyLlama as its default model, with BioMistral-7B~ labrak2024biomistral available as an alternative where the latency tradeoff is acceptable. On the retrieval side, hybrid search combining sparse ( bm25)~ robertson2009bm25 and dense~ reimers2019sbert methods consistently outperforms either method alone on open-domain qa benchmarks~ ma2022hybrid. The fusion method used here is Reciprocal Rank Fusion ( rrf)~ cormack2009rrf, which merges ranked lists without requiring score normalisation across retrieval systems. A qa system that gives no indication of its own uncertainty is potentially harmful --- a confidently wrong answer may be acted upon. Multi-signal confidence estimation~ guo2017calibration gives the system a mechanism to communicate when it is less certain. The five signals used here cover retrieval quality, generation consistency, source agreement, nli-based hallucination detection~ he2021deberta, and medical entity coverage. These are combined with Platt scaling~ platt1999probabilistic to produce a calibrated confidence score. Motivation The immediate motivation came from a straightforward observation: patients asking about their own health conditions rarely get answers tailored to their situation from general search engines. Results for a query like ``knee swelling after a fall'' are written for a broad audience, padded with disclaimers, and stripped of specificity. A retrieval system indexed on real patient-doctor conversation data has a reasonable chance of matching a specific question pattern to a previously answered case with similar clinical context. There is also an academic angle. rag for general domains is well studied, but healthcare-specific rag with explicit confidence signals and hallucination detection is less common. Most published medical qa systems report accuracy on structured benchmarks such as MedQA~ jin2021medqa or PubMedQA~ jin2019pubmedqa without reporting calibration or uncertainty estimates. A system that says ``80 % confident'' is more useful than one that says ``here is the answer'' --- provided that 80 % is actually calibrated to observed accuracy. The technical interest centred on running this entire stack on cpu. Most published rag systems assume gpu availability for retrieval and generation alike. Making bm25 and dense retrieval and llm generation work at acceptable quality on a machine without a gpu required specific choices: bm25 caching via pickle file, lru caching for embedding lookups, and gguf quantisation for the 7B model. Scope of the project Functional scope The system accepts free-text medical questions in English and returns an answer grounded in retrieved documents, with source attribution, a confidence score, and a standard safety disclaimer. Four query types are handled: factual definitions, symptom-based questions, research questions drawn from PubMedQA abstracts, and clinical questions from MedMCQA. Questions involving prescription dosages, emergency triage, or content caught by the safety guardrail are redirected to clinical care rather than answered directly. Non-functional scope The system runs without a gpu and without internet access at query time. User queries are not logged or stored. Response latency averages 106 seconds on a warm path on the test hardware, which places it outside the range of real-time consumer applications. This is a research prototype; latency and output quality would both improve substantially on gpu hardware or with a more specialised model. Technical scope The project covers knowledge base construction, hybrid retrieval, generation, xai confidence scoring, hallucination detection, a REST api layer, and a React-based frontend. It does not cover clinical validation, regulatory compliance, or multi-turn conversation memory. The knowledge base is static once built; there is no mechanism for continuous ingestion of new literature. Assumptions and constraints All development and local evaluation ran on cpu-only hardware. gpu compute was used for two tasks: QLoRA fine-tuning, which ran on a Google Colab tpu v5e instance, and BioMistral evaluation, which ran on a Colab T4. All three source datasets are publicly available for research use. No proprietary or patient-identifiable data was used at any point in the project.
